Exploring an essential question across five major religious traditions!

1

Choose a Question

A good question deserves a thorough answer! Choose one of the five options below and keep it in front of you. It will tell you what to read closely and what to skip. There are no one-word answers to these inquiries.

  1. How does the study of religion help us to gain empathy and understanding of the world around us?
  2. How does religion affect the lives of its followers?
  3. Do the lasting impacts of religion outweigh the enduring issues?
  4. Does religion create more unity or division?
  5. Is there a “right” or “wrong” way to use religion?

Who Counts as “Major”?

This assignment covers Buddhism, Christianity, Hinduism, Islam, and Judaism. That's a common way to organize the subject, but not the only one. About 14.8 million people worldwide are Jewish; that's about 0.2 percent of humanity. About 25 million people are Sikh, yet Sikhism rarely makes the list. So what makes a religion “major”? Its size? Its age? Its influence on other traditions? Scholars still debate the question. As you research, think about why we're focusing on these five and consider who's left out.

2

Get the Big Picture

Start with an encyclopedia! Britannica School and World Book will provide basic overviews of each religion and some useful vocabulary you'll need to search deeper. Read for breadth, not depth. Expect some traditions to buck the template: Hinduism, for example, has no single founder. When a category doesn't fit, explain why!

  • school.eb.comBritannica School

    Articles, images, videos, and primary sources, most available at three reading levels. A good place to start a search.

  • worldbookonline.comWorld Book Online

    Thousands of articles with illustrations, videos, and interactive maps. A great surface-level source, but not a final destination.

Cite as you go

Create a NoodleTools project before you start reading. In World Book, scroll to the bottom of the article, click How to cite this article, then Export to NoodleTools. In Britannica School, click Cite, then Export to NoodleTools. Either way, check every field against the article. Software might make mistakes, but you're the one earning a grade that reflects them!

3

Go Deeper

Now, narrow your search. Let your essential question choose your search terms. World Religions covers beliefs, practices, and history in depth. Explora adds magazines and newspapers, useful when your project calls for a current source.

  • religion.abc-clio.comWorld Religions

    More than 50 religions, past and present: important figures, core beliefs, customs and practices, holidays, sacred places, and daily updates.

  • research.ebsco.comExplora High Schools

    Built for high school researchers: age-appropriate magazines and newspapers, Topic Overviews to start you off, and 60,000 Associated Press videos. Try the Topic Explorer pages!

Cite as you go

Both databases export to NoodleTools. In World Religions, click Cite at the top of the article and choose Export to NoodleTools. In Explora, use the article's export tools to send it to NoodleTools. If nothing happens, check your pop-up blocker or fall back on Quick Cite. Then compare what arrived with the article itself: author, title, date, and URL.

4

Find Texts and Objects

Encyclopedias describe a religion. Sacred texts, art, and objects let you meet it on its own terms. Use these sources to find a passage, an image, or an artifact for your project.

  • Storybook

    Illustrate with care. Some traditions avoid images of sacred figures. Many Muslims consider any depiction of the Prophet Muhammad disrespectful, though some historical Islamic manuscripts show him. When in doubt, use calligraphy, architecture, or symbols.

  • Slides

    Use two of the DBQ's documents, then find one current source of your own. Explora's magazines and newspapers are a good place to start.

  • Shoebox Museum

    Museums label every object: what it is, where and when it was made, and where it lives now. Borrow that habit!

Overviews

  • Harvard UniversityThe Pluralism Project

    Harvard's Pluralism Project profiles 17 traditions and studies religious life in the United States.

Art, Objects, and Symbols

  • SmarthistorySmarthistory

    Free art history by scholars, including essays and videos on sacred art and architecture.

  • The Metropolitan Museum of ArtHeilbrunn Timeline of Art History

    The Met's essays on art across time and place. Start with “Buddhism and Buddhist Art” and “Figural Representation in Islamic Art.”

  • Smithsonian National Museum of Asian ArtEncountering Religion in Asian Art

    The Smithsonian's guide to objects from Hindu, Buddhist, and Islamic traditions, written for teachers and students.

Sacred Texts

Read a tradition's texts in its own words. Translations differ, and so do traditions: Christians, for instance, disagree about which books belong in the Bible. Whatever you read, note which translation you're using.

  • SefariaSefaria

    A free nonprofit library of Jewish texts, from the Torah to the Talmud, in Hebrew and English.

  • SuttaCentralSuttaCentral

    Early Buddhist scriptures in their original languages, with free modern translations. It focuses on the earliest period of Buddhism.

  • Quran.FoundationQuran.com

    The Qur'an in Arabic, with translations in many languages, from the nonprofit Quran.Foundation.

  • HarperCollins Christian PublishingBible Gateway

    Dozens of English translations, Protestant and Catholic, side by side. Owned by HarperCollins Christian Publishing. Try the NRSVue, an ecumenical translation edited by the Society of Biblical Literature and used across Protestant, Catholic, and Orthodox churches.

  • Indian Institute of Technology KanpurGita Supersite

    From the Indian Institute of Technology Kanpur: the Bhagavad Gita in Sanskrit with English and Hindi translations and classical commentaries. Sister sites hold the Upanishads and the Ramayana.

Numbers

  • Pew Research CenterHow the Global Religious Landscape Changed From 2010 to 2020

    How many people follow each religion, where they live, and how that changed from 2010 to 2020, drawn from more than 2,700 censuses and surveys.

Cite as you go

Most of these sites won't export to NoodleTools, so build these citations yourself: author (if there is one), title, website, publisher, date, and URL. For a sacred text, record the translation. Two translations of the same verse can read very differently, and your reader deserves to know which one you used.

5

Cite It

Every source you used belongs in your Works Cited, and every quotation, paraphrase, and image needs an in-text citation. Where does that go in a storybook or a shoebox museum? Try a Sources page at the back of the book or a credit line on each label. Ask for help if you're not sure how to place them.
